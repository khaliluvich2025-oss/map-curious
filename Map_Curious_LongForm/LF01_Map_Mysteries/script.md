## PART 01 — Cold open (0:00–0:30)

[curious] There's a waterfall taller than any on land, and nobody has ever seen it. [short pause] There's a town in Pennsylvania that has been burning underground since 1962. And there's a man who jumped out of a passenger jet with two hundred thousand dollars, and was never found. [confident] These places sound made up. They're all real, and they're all on the map. Today, we're visiting six of them. And the last one didn't even exist until 1963.

## PART 02 — #6 The Hudson River (≈1:10)

[curious] Let's start somewhere almost everyone has seen. [short pause] The Hudson River. It begins high in New York's Adirondack Mountains and runs south, past Albany, all the way to New York City. On a map, it looks like any other river. Water goes downhill, to the sea. Simple. [confident] Except the lower half of the Hudson isn't really a river. It's an estuary, an arm of the ocean reaching inland. Twice a day, the tide pushes in from New York Harbor, and the current turns around and flows north. [short pause] That tide reaches about a hundred and fifty miles upstream, all the way to the dam at Troy, near Albany. And in dry summers, when less fresh water comes down from the mountains, salty ocean water can creep about sixty miles up the river. [curious] People here noticed long before maps were drawn. One Native name for the Hudson is often translated as "the river that flows both ways." So that's a river that runs backward twice a day. Next, a fortress that ended up in the middle of nowhere.

## PART 03 — #5 Fort Jefferson (≈1:20)

[curious] About seventy miles west of Key West, past the end of the road, there's a tiny cluster of islands called the Dry Tortugas. [short pause] And on one of them sits one of the largest brick forts in the Americas. ⚑ [confident] Fort Jefferson was started in 1846. The United States wanted to control the shipping lanes between the Gulf of Mexico and the Atlantic, and this spot sat right beside them. Workers hauled in millions of bricks, more than sixteen million by most counts, ⚑ and kept building for about thirty years. [serious] But the fort was never finished. New rifled cannons could smash brick walls, and the heavy fort was settling into the island beneath it. [short pause] During and after the Civil War, the remote fort became a prison. Its most famous inmate was Dr. Samuel Mudd, convicted after he set the broken leg of Lincoln's assassin, John Wilkes Booth. [curious] Today it's the heart of Dry Tortugas National Park, reachable only by boat or seaplane. A giant fortress, built for a war that never came to it. Now, let's head underground.

## PART 04 — #4 Centralia (≈1:20)

[curious] In eastern Pennsylvania, there's a town where the ground is on fire. [short pause] Centralia sits on top of a coal seam. In 1962, a fire started in the area, possibly while a dump was being burned, and it crept into the old coal mines beneath the town. [serious] Underground, the fire found an endless supply of fuel. Smoke and dangerous gases began rising through cracks in yards and roads. In 1981, a boy fell into a steaming sinkhole that opened beneath him. He survived, but the danger was now impossible to ignore. [confident] In the 1980s, the federal government funded a buyout, and nearly all of the roughly one thousand residents moved away. ⚑ Most of the houses were torn down. Even the town's ZIP code was later taken away. [curious] Today, Centralia is mostly empty streets and woods. And the fire is still burning, slowly moving through the coal. Some estimates say it could keep going for another two hundred years. ⚑ From a fire under the ground, to a man who fell out of the sky.

## PART 05 — #3 D.B. Cooper (≈1:30)

[curious] On November 24th, 1971, a man in a dark suit boarded a Northwest Orient flight from Portland, Oregon, to Seattle. [short pause] He bought his ticket under the name Dan Cooper. Once the plane was in the air, he handed a flight attendant a note. He had a bomb. [serious] In Seattle, he traded the passengers for two hundred thousand dollars and four parachutes. Then he ordered the crew to take off again and fly south, toward Mexico, low and slow. Somewhere over southwest Washington, in the dark and the rain, he lowered the rear stairs, and jumped. [short pause] He was never seen again. [confident] The FBI searched forests and rivers for years. In 1980, a boy digging along the Columbia River found about fifty-eight hundred dollars of the ransom money, still in rotting bundles. The serial numbers matched. [curious] But no body, no parachute, and no Cooper. In 2016, the FBI stopped actively investigating the case. It remains the only unsolved airplane hijacking in American history. ⚑ Whether he survived that jump is still an open question. Next, we go to the bottom of the sea.

## PART 06 — #2 The Denmark Strait Cataract (≈1:30)

[curious] Between Greenland and Iceland lies a channel of the North Atlantic called the Denmark Strait. [short pause] From above, it looks like ordinary ocean. But beneath the surface is the largest waterfall on Earth. [confident] Here's how it works. North of the strait, in the Nordic Seas, the water is extremely cold. Cold water is denser and heavier than warm water. As this cold water flows south, it meets the warmer, lighter water of the Atlantic. [short pause] Instead of mixing, the heavy cold water sinks beneath it. Then it reaches the edge of an undersea ridge, and spills down the slope toward the deep ocean floor, dropping roughly two miles. ⚑ [serious] It's not water falling through air, like Niagara. It's water falling through water. But the volume is enormous, far more than any river or waterfall on land. ⚑ [curious] And it matters far beyond Greenland. This cold, sinking water helps drive the currents that move heat around the planet. A waterfall nobody can see, quietly shaping the world's oceans. And now, for our number one: a place that didn't exist until 1963.

## PART 07 — #1 Surtsey (≈1:40)

[curious] In November 1963, fishermen off Iceland's south coast spotted dark smoke rising from the sea. [short pause] At first, they thought a ship might be on fire. It wasn't a ship. An underwater volcano was erupting. [confident] Within a day, a brand-new island broke the surface. Iceland named it Surtsey, after a fire giant from Norse mythology. The eruptions continued until 1967. By then, the island covered about one square mile. [curious] And then, something unusual happened: almost nothing. Scientists decided to leave Surtsey alone. Only researchers are allowed to land, and they work carefully not to bring seeds or insects with them. [short pause] That turned Surtsey into a natural laboratory. Scientists have watched life arrive on bare rock: seeds carried by the sea and wind, insects, and birds, which brought nutrients and more seeds. Then plants took hold. Today, Surtsey is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. [serious] But the sea is winning. Waves keep wearing away the soft rock, and about half of Surtsey's original area is already gone. The island that was born in front of us is slowly disappearing.

## PART 08 — Wrap and community CTA (≈0:30)

[confident] So that's six places that sound made up, but are real. A river that flows both ways. A fortress at the end of the world. A town burning from below. A hijacker who vanished. A waterfall under the sea. And an island younger than many of the people watching this. [curious] Every map is full of stories like these.
